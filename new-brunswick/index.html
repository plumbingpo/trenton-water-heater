How New Brunswick Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

Many early failures trace back to maintenance that never happened, and the habits that prevent them are simple. Flush the tank once a year to clear sediment, get the anode rod checked every few years, and test the relief valve annually so it doesn't seize. Set the thermostat close to 120 degrees for safer water and less scale. For a tankless heater in a hard-water area, plan on descaling once a year to protect the heat exchanger.

Equipment age plays a big role. With regular service, a tank water heater usually runs 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts showing warning signs, like rust-colored water, popping noises, or moisture around the base, it's time to plan a replacement. A planned replacement costs less and causes far less stress than an emergency swap with water spreading across the floor.

Left alone, a small problem tends to grow. Consider a spent anode rod: replacing it is a quick, inexpensive repair, but once it's gone, the water starts eating the tank steel, and suddenly a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we encourage homeowners to save our number in their phone and reach out at the first sign of trouble. Catching it early saves time and money and prevents a surprise cleanup.

Common Water Heater Problems in New Brunswick

Around New Brunswick, water heater trouble usually trace back to a handful of causes. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be part of the picture: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the popping and rumbling you may hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling is worth keeping up with in hard-water areas.

Aging equipment is the other half of the story. A standard tank water heater typically lasts 8 to 12 years, and near the end of that window the anode rod is often eaten away, leaving the steel open to rust. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Code requirements add another layer. Swapping out an older heater frequently means updating the installation along the way: a thermal expansion tank if the water system is closed, a T&P discharge line that terminates correctly, venting that drafts properly on gas units, plus straps and a pan where required. Well water brings its own challenges, such as extra sediment, iron staining, and the sulfur odor that sometimes develops when the water reacts with a standard anode rod. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most are much cheaper when caught early. If something seems off, a quick diagnostic visit catches the small problem before it becomes an expensive one.
