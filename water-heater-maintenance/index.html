If you could see inside your water heater, the case for maintenance would make itself. On the bottom there is a bed of mineral sediment, sometimes several inches deep, that makes the burner work harder, causes that familiar rumbling, and can burn out the lower element on an electric unit. Hanging from the top is the anode rod, meant to corrode first, which may be down to bare wire after a few years. When it is used up, corrosion goes after the tank lining, and a leak is only a matter of time. Across Trenton, NJ, a yearly visit is the simplest way to keep both of those problems from shortening the life of the heater.

Our maintenance visit is built to interrupt both problems. We drain and flush the tank until the water runs clear, pull and inspect the anode rod and recommend a replacement when it is mostly consumed, and test the temperature and pressure relief valve to confirm it opens and reseats. On gas units we inspect the burner, pilot or igniter, thermocouple or flame sensor, flue baffle, and venting for proper draft. On electric units we check element resistance and thermostat operation and look at the wiring for heat damage. We check the expansion tank's air charge against your water pressure, confirm the thermostat setting is near 120 degrees to limit scalding, and look for early leaks at fittings and valves.

Tankless water heaters have their own maintenance needs. Scale builds up inside the heat exchanger, especially in hard water, and eventually triggers error codes, reduced flow, or temperature swings. We descale the heat exchanger with a pump and descaling solution through the service valves, clean the inlet filter, check the venting and condensate line on condensing models, and review the unit's error history. When to Call for Water Heater Maintenance

Water heaters usually give plenty of warning before they fail, as long as you know what to look and listen for. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • Energy bills creeping up with no change in hot water use
  • Rusty or discolored hot water at the tap
  • Small drips at the drain valve or connections
  • A tank that pops and knocks during a heating cycle
  • Shorter showers before the water turns lukewarm
  • No memory of the last time the water heater was serviced
  • A rotten-egg smell from the hot water
  • A tankless unit showing error codes or fluctuating temperatures

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Not every maintenance task is a good DIY project. Keeping clutter away from the heater, checking for leaks, and noticing new noises are all useful. Flushing, anode replacement, relief valve testing, and burner or element work are another story. Hot water, seized fittings, brittle drain valves, and sediment that clogs the valve midway through can turn a simple job into a flooded floor. Relief valves on older heaters sometimes fail to close after testing. And gas-side checks involve flame quality and venting, which affect your household's air.

Our technicians carry the tools and parts for every step: replacement drain valves, anode rods sized for your tank, flexible anodes for tight clearances, T&P valves, and meters for testing elements and thermostats. For tankless systems, we bring the pump and descaling setup. Just as important, we know what to look for, from early corrosion at the fittings to a thermocouple starting to weaken, so you can fix small things before it becomes a failure. The goal is simple: a heater that lasts longer, runs more efficiently, and does not surprise you with a leak.

Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

A few preventive steps can help you avoid a cold shower or a wet floor down the road. Here are our recommendations:

  • Ask about the anode rod at each visit; it is the tank's main defense against rust
  • Ask about water treatment if flushing turns up heavy mineral buildup
  • Stay on top of tankless descaling to avoid error codes and reduced flow
  • Check the drain pan and fittings every few months for drips or rust
  • Do not store boxes, paint, or chemicals against the water heater
  • Avoid turning the tank up past 120 degrees unless a mixing valve is installed
  • Schedule a flush every year, or every six months if you have hard water
